Summer is the perfect time to ask a question coffee drinkers have strong opinions about: **cold brew or pour over?** Both methods can produce exceptional cups from the same beans. But they do very different things to flavour — and once you understand why, you'll never brew on autopilot again.

**The short answer:** pour over reveals more complexity. Cold brew reveals more comfort. The longer answer is more interesting — and it comes down to temperature, time, and what each method is actually extracting from your beans.

![Cold brew vs pour over infographic — brew temperature, time, body, acidity, and best Volcano Drip roast pairings](/blog/coldbrew-pourover-infographic.png)

## The science: why temperature changes everything

Hot water (ideally 90–96°C for pour over) is an aggressive solvent. It rapidly extracts acids, aromatic compounds, and bright volatile flavours from ground coffee. The result is a cup that's complex, dynamic, and full of the origin characteristics that make single-origin coffee worth drinking in the first place — citrus, stone fruit, florals, the mineral edge you get from volcanic soil.

Cold water extracts slowly and selectively. Over 12–24 hours, it pulls out the sugars and heavier flavour compounds while leaving most of the acidic elements behind. The result is smooth, low-acid, and naturally sweeter — but you lose some of the delicate high notes that make a light roast extraordinary.

> Temperature isn't just a preference — it determines which flavours your water unlocks.

## What each method does to single-origin flavour

### Pour over: maximum transparency

Pour over is a high-resolution method. It shows you exactly what a coffee is. The controlled pour, the bloom, the draw-down — each step influences extraction in ways you can taste. With a single-origin light or medium roast, a good pour over will give you distinct tasting notes: not just "coffee" but the specific character of that origin.

With our [Primera Luz](/origins/costa-rica) from Costa Rica, a pour over brings out a clean citrus brightness with honey sweetness and a milk chocolate finish — exactly as the bean was grown and roasted to express. The volcanic soil of the Tarrazú region shows through.

With our [Inca Ascent](/origins/peru) from Peru, pour over lifts the soft citrus and stone fruit notes while the subtle cocoa sits quietly in the background. It's the kind of cup that rewards paying attention.

### Cold brew: maximum comfort

Cold brew is a low-resolution, high-enjoyment method. It softens the edges. Acidity drops, bitterness fades, and the natural sweetness of the bean comes forward in a way hot brewing rarely achieves. If you find pour over too sharp or tart, cold brew from the same beans can feel like a different coffee entirely.

This is where darker, bolder roasts shine. Our [Sumatra Black](/origins/indonesia) — grown at 3,404m on the slopes of the Barisan mountains — was practically made for cold brew. The earthy depth, the dark chocolate backbone, the subtle smokiness: cold water amplifies the rich base notes and turns the result into something that drinks almost like a dessert without being sweet.

[Antigua Ember](/origins/guatemala) from Guatemala is another excellent cold brew candidate. The cocoa and caramel notes that define this medium roast become even more pronounced when extracted cold and slow.

## The roast match-up

A useful general rule: **the lighter the roast, the more you benefit from pour over. The darker the roast, the more cold brew rewards you.** Here's how the Volcano Drip lineup lines up:

- **Primera Luz (Costa Rica, Light)** — Pour over. The citrus and honey notes need heat to fully express.
- **Inca Ascent (Peru, Medium)** — Pour over first, cold brew second. Versatile, but complexity shines hot.
- **Antigua Ember (Guatemala, Medium)** — Either method. The caramel base works beautifully cold or hot.
- **Copán Rise (Honduras, Medium)** — Either method. Balanced and smooth in both formats.
- **Sumatra Black (Indonesia, Dark)** — Cold brew. Heat can push the smokiness too far; cold water keeps it balanced.

## Grind matters too

Cold brew requires a coarser grind than pour over — think French press territory. Too fine and the long steeping time over-extracts, making the concentrate bitter rather than smooth. Pour over sits in the medium-fine range, where the controlled pour and short contact time do the work.

## Which should you choose?

If it's a warm day and you want something refreshing with depth, make cold brew from [Sumatra Black](/origins/indonesia) or [Antigua Ember](/origins/guatemala). Start it the night before, strain it in the morning, and serve over ice. It keeps in the fridge for a week.

If you want to actually taste your coffee — to understand what makes a Costa Rica different from a Peru — make a pour over. Kettle, scale, three minutes of attention. You'll taste things you've never noticed before.

The good news: you don't have to pick. Both methods belong in your rotation. Different days call for different rituals.

> The best brewing method is the one that fits the moment — and the bean.
